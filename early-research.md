# Early Research Module: From Guidelines and Current Issues to the Common Thread

Contents: purpose, steps 1-6, research sheet (output format), common thread, data rules.

## Purpose
The title, introduction, and discussion only connect if all of them come from the same starting material. This module gathers that material before writing, so the theme, guidelines, current problem, data, and solution gap are not searched for again in every section. The result is one "research sheet" used by all later stages.

## Steps

### 1. Read the guidelines and theme
Take from the user or from the guidelines file: competition name, theme, subtheme, page/word limit, format, section order, rubric, rules on figures, and rules on AI use or disclosure. If there is a rubric, note the heaviest weight; the rubric overrides the skill's assumptions. If the guidelines impose limits that conflict with this skill, note them and follow the guidelines.

### 2. Map current issues within the subtheme
Find a problem that is real right now within the chosen subtheme: news from the last 12 months, official agency reports, policies recently issued or under discussion. Use a web search tool when available and record the access date; for anything that changes (figures, regulations, prices, who holds an office), always check and do not rely on memory. Choose one problem that has data, has a local case, and has not been fully handled.

### 3. Collect the core data
Target: 1-2 national data points, 1 local data point or case from the same or a nearby year, and 1 data point on the consequence if the problem is left alone. Main sources: BPS (Statistics Indonesia), ministries, regional offices, official institutions, reputable journals. Source years are the last 5 years counted from today. Data that cannot be found is marked [DATA NEEDED: ...], not invented.

### 4. Map existing solutions and their gaps
Make a short list: existing solutions or efforts, who runs them, and which part they do not reach. This produces the "point of difference" for B4 and the "shortcomings of previous solutions" for the introduction. If no similar idea turns up, search again with other keywords before stating that none exists.

### 5. Check related regulations and policies
If the idea touches rules (bans, programs, standards, permits), record the correct name and number of each rule from an official source. These are used in the policy feasibility part (B5) and in the introduction. Never write a regulation number from memory.

### 6. Choose the idea and test it
Test the idea on four points: it is an idea (not research), it fits the theme/subtheme, local materials or potential are available, and it has one goal that can be quantified as a projection. If it passes, move on to the title.

## The common thread (continuity)
One formulation reused in every section, in the same terms:

Problem → shortcomings of the old solution → idea → how it works → advantages → impact → projection

Rules of use:
- The title keywords (name of the idea, the problem, the goal) reappear in P5 of the introduction, in B1, in B8, and in the closing.
- The data in the introduction becomes the comparison figure when B6 calculates potential impact.
- The challenges in B5 are answered by a step in B3 or a stage in B7.
- Each technical term gets one name and keeps it for the whole manuscript.
- Before handing over, write the common thread in one sentence and check whether each section supports it.

## Data rules
Follow the "Data and honesty rules" section in SKILL.md. One important claim, one citation. Figures from other people's studies are written as those studies' findings, and benefit figures for the idea are written as projections.
