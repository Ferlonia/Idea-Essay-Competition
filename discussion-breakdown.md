# Discussion Breakdown: A Neutral Framework from Winning-Style Essays

Contents: how to read this file, the eight discussion blocks (B1-B8), neutral sentence patterns, common mistakes, mapping to the older skill's items.

## How to read this file

The material is the mentor's notes on two example essays: one system/device idea essay (broken down by content "point", slides Point 3 to Point 7) and one product idea essay (broken down with yellow highlights per section). All product names, materials, and specific problems from the examples have been replaced by neutral markers:

- [IDEA] = the name of the idea or product being written about
- [PROBLEM] = the problem raised in the title
- [OLD WAY] = the way currently used, or the existing solution
- [MATERIAL/TOOL] = the core material, component, or technology
- [TARGET] = the user group or location of application

Slides Point 1 and Point 2 are not in the photos. B1 and B2 below were assembled from the older skill and from the "innovation concept" part of the product essay, so they are marked [ADDED].

Each block has: the message to the judge (what the judge must believe after reading it), the function of each paragraph, a fitting table or figure, and the traps. The mentor marked each paragraph with a color and one function; follow that principle: one paragraph, one job.

All tables in the examples use the same pattern: the title "Table n. ..." above, a source line below ("Source: processed by the author (year)" or "processed by the author from Name (year)"), and each table is cited in the paragraph before it ("presented in Table 2"). Use this pattern for tables and figures.

## B1. Idea overview [ADDED]
Message to the judge: the idea has a clear form and a clear difference.
- P1: a one-paragraph definition: [IDEA] is a [form] made from/based on [MATERIAL/TOOL] designed to [goal in the title] for [TARGET]. In the product essay, the mentor asked for this part to carry the subheading "[IDEA]: Innovation Concept" and to contain the definition of the idea.
- P2 (if needed): one sentence on why this approach was chosen over the common approach ([OLD WAY]).

## B2. Fit with the theme and subtheme [ADDED]
Message to the judge: the idea sits inside the theme.
- One short paragraph that names the theme and subtheme in the organizers' terms, then shows which part of the design answers it. Do not pile up claims; one strong link is more useful than four thin ones.

## B3. How it works and the design (Point 3)
Message to the judge: the idea can really be run in the field; it does not stop on paper.
- P1, general workflow: the sequence of steps from inputs to the result being used, and the cycle if it repeats. Pair it with one flow figure (see visuals.md).
- P2, tools, materials, and components: name what is used, why simple or easy-to-get ones are enough, and who can make or run it. Pair it with a labeled design figure if the idea is a device. In the product essay, this part contains target users, the list of tools and materials, the making procedure, and the benefit or function of each material supported by relevant research.
- P3, processing or implementation steps: sequential from start to finish, with a note that no high expertise is needed if that is true. Pair it with a Stage | Activity | Purpose table (4-6 rows).
- P4, design advantage: one clever design feature, such as a by-product being reused, low cost, or suitability for areas with limited resources.
- Parties involved: if the idea needs many parties, show it with a stakeholder scheme (example in the product essay: the pentahelix model with five elements: academia, business, community, media, government).
- Prototype: the product essay attaches a prototype image (for example packaging) and explains its unique feature, for example a QR code carrying educational content.
Trap: explaining the material's theory at length while how it works cannot be pictured.

## B4. Advantages and differentiators (Point 4)
Message to the judge: [IDEA] is better than [OLD WAY] and different from existing ideas, so its originality is immediately visible.
- P1, main advantage: [IDEA] works on the cause of [PROBLEM], while [OLD WAY] only works after [PROBLEM] has happened. Close by pointing to the comparison table.
- Comparison table: columns Aspect | [OLD WAY] | [IDEA], 4-6 aspects measured the same way on both sides (for example risk, material treatment, impact, cost, economic value). The source is written "processed by the author from ..." with a citation.
- P2, evidence of benefit: studies that support the benefit claim (meta-analyses, systematic reviews, agency reports), with figures and citations. Write figures as the findings of other people's studies, not as the author's test results.
- P3, where it differs: "Until now [MATERIAL/APPROACH] has been used as X. [IDEA] uses it as Y." One sentence naming the gap being filled, plus a short competitor analysis if a similar idea exists.
Trap: a one-sided comparison table (only advantageous aspects chosen) or a differentiator that is only a new name.

## B5. Feasibility (Point 5)
Message to the judge: the idea is realistic and can be applied.
- P1, material, technology, and social feasibility: the material is available in [TARGET] without being brought from outside; the tool is simple and fits local conditions; implementation can be collective through a group or cooperative so the burden is shared.
- P2, economic feasibility: the main cost sits in one component; say why it is cheap; one unit can serve many people; by-products reduce other expenses. A simple calculation is fine with the assumptions written out and a price source; without a source, mark [DATA NEEDED].
- P3, policy feasibility: the idea aligns with the direction of a government regulation or program, named with the correct name and number, and answers a real question in the field ("if the old way is banned, what replaces it?"). The policy support meant: tools, training, incentives.
- P4, challenges and how to overcome them: the author admits the real challenges (training, time, habit change), then shows they can all be managed with mentoring and support. This part doubles as the weakness and threat sides of a SWOT analysis; if a SWOT is needed, a compact 2x2 table is enough.
- The product essay places the weakness honestly at the end of the discussion: further research is needed (side effects, dosage, consumption feasibility) and collaboration among parties. This pattern may be used; judges read a measured admission as a sign that the author understands the limits of the idea.
Trap: feasibility that consists only of the words "cheap and easy" without a single figure, source, or assumption.

## B6. Impact and link to development goals (Point 6)
Message to the judge: the idea's benefits are wider than the title's goal, and its contribution is specific.
- P1, layered impact: detail it from three sides (for example environment, related sectors, economy), then unify them under one umbrella concept (for example circular economy: waste becomes a resource). Pair it with a Goal (SDGs) | Form of contribution table, 3-4 rows, each contribution specific; a goal number alone is not enough.
- P2, large-scale impact: use data on the scale of the problem to show the potential if applied widely. Case data must be recent; old figures only with a reason.
- P3, social and health impact: who feels it directly, especially vulnerable groups; connect it to daily life.
Rule: use this part if the theme or organizers touch on SDGs or impact. It belongs in the body, not in the title. The one main goal in the title does not change because of this table.

## B7. Development projection (Point 7)
Message to the judge: there is a path from small scale to wider impact without losing footing in field conditions.
- A Stage | Target | Form of development table, three rows: short term (1-2 years, one village/group/agency, pilot and training), medium term (3-5 years, several locations, replication through cooperatives, communities, or partnerships), long term (over 5 years, region or policy level, integration with larger schemes).
- P1, gradual development: explain the table in plain language; the idea may sound futuristic but its first step is small and realistic.
- P2, additional opportunities: if an official scheme gives economic value or incentives (for example carbon trading, a government program partnership), state it as a potential ("has the potential"), give the scheme's source, and do not calculate income without data.
- P3, restating the direction of the idea: close with the core: a clear path, who leads, and an old habit turned into small steps that pay off.

## B8. Statement of innovation and creativity
Message to the judge: this is what is new, this is why it is different, this is the measurable benefit.
- Combine B4 (differentiator), B6 (impact), and B7 (direction) in one short paragraph at the end of the discussion. State three things explicitly: what is new, why it differs from what exists, what the benefit is (sourced projection).

## Neutral sentence patterns
Replace the markers; do not change the structure. These patterns deliberately avoid "not just X, but Y", "in addition", and the opening words on the style banned list.
- Differentiator: "Until now [MATERIAL/APPROACH] has been used for X. [IDEA] uses it for Y, and no other idea has filled that gap yet."
- Advantage: "[OLD WAY] only works after [PROBLEM] has happened. [IDEA] works on the cause."
- Feasibility: "The main material already exists in [TARGET], the tool can be built from cheap [MATERIAL/TOOL], and the work is shared through a [group/cooperative]."
- Challenge: "The biggest challenge is [training/time/habit]. Mentoring and policy support answer it, so the obstacle is technical and manageable."
- Table reference: "A comparison of the two is presented in Table n."
- Transition between blocks: one sentence that links to the next block, not needed in every paragraph.

## Common mistakes
1. How it works cannot be pictured: no sequence of steps or figure.
2. A one-sided or unsourced comparison.
3. Benefit figures from other people's studies written as if they were the author's test results (this slips into research style).
4. Feasibility without assumptions and sources.
5. Tables and figures not cited in the text or not numbered in order (example: Figure 1, then Figure 3).
6. SDGs impact as a list of numbers without a specific contribution.
7. A projection without a time frame and a target.
