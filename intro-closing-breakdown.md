# Breakdown of Title, Introduction, and Closing (mentor annotations, neutralized)

Contents: title annotation, introduction paragraph functions, merging with the 5-paragraph pattern, closing, critical notes from the photos, title types judges like.

The source is one product-type idea essay that the mentor highlighted section by section in yellow (pages 2/7 to 7/7). The product name, materials, and specific figures are not used; only their function is. Markers: [IDEA], [PROBLEM], [OLD WAY], [MATERIAL/TOOL], [TARGET] (the same meaning as in discussion-breakdown.md).

## Title annotation
The mentor gave two notes on the title: a maximum of 20 words, and the title must show the idea and the problem discussed. The author also labeled the idea's name "Essay idea/innovation" and the problem part below it "Problem". So the judge reads the title as two things that must be visible at once: the idea and the problem. If the 20-word note differs from the competition's limit, follow the guidelines; if the guidelines are silent, treat 20 words as the safe target and 25 as the ceiling (see titles.md).

## Introduction paragraph functions (from the mentor's highlights)
The functions that were highlighted, in order from start to end:
1. The main idea and the author's argument about the problem discussed. It may open with one quotation if the quotation is real and verifiable (source and speaker clear); without that, open with a statement of the problem.
2. The argument is followed by relevant data or facts. Working pattern: one claim, one cited data point, one more claim. Use national data first, then comparison data or another case, until the scale is visible.
3. The shortcomings of previous solutions. Name the kinds of effort that already exist (for example prevention, supervision, enforcement, education), then show who or what part they do not reach, with data.
4. A statement of the opening to break the problem: "a solution is needed that is [criterion: cheap / easy to reach / based on local potential]". These criteria are later tested in B5 (feasibility).
5. One potential that can serve as the solution, its content or characteristics, and the relevant research that supports it. One potential only; if there are two materials, explain each with one citation.
6. A transition sentence that states the idea in full: "The author proposes [IDEA] to [goal in the title] in [TARGET]." Right after it, go straight into the DISCUSSION (PEMBAHASAN).

## Merging with the 5-paragraph pattern
| Paragraph (older skill) | Mentor function that goes in |
|---|---|
| P1 general context and national data | 1 and 2 |
| P2 local data and case | 2 (continued: comparison data or case) |
| P3 urgency and consequences | 3 (shortcomings of previous solutions that keep the problem alive) |
| P4 existing solutions and their gap, arrival of the idea | 4 and 5 |
| P5 strength of the idea, focus, direction, transition | 6 |

If the page limit is tight, merge P1 and P2 into one data paragraph and keep functions 3, 4-5, and 6 as one paragraph each.

## Closing
Pattern from the last page of the example essay (section title: "Kesimpulan dan Saran", Conclusion and Recommendations):
- Return the key figure or fact about the problem once, then state that the real problem runs deeper than that figure (if that is true for your idea).
- State [IDEA] as the solution, with the same one-sentence definition as the title (the title's keywords appear again).
- End with the author's hope: the idea does not stop as a written work and can be developed and applied in [TARGET].
- Recommendations: point to the parties who can follow up (researchers, government, community), concrete and short. The mentor marked the closing with a single label ("Conclusion and recommendations"), so it need not be lengthened; do not introduce new data or arguments.

## Critical notes from the photos (so they are not copied wholesale)
1. The example title has 24 words (counted by spaces), longer than the mentor's note on the same page (20 maximum), and contains an acronym expansion in parentheses and a scientific name. That conflicts with the rules in titles.md. The example is good for its paragraph functions, not for the form of its title.
2. Figure numbering jumps (Figure 1, then Figure 3). Your manuscript must be sequential and every figure must be cited in the text.
3. Claims about a material's benefits use strong language ("safe for the body", "can overcome") that depends on other parties' research. Write them with references and verbs that match the strength of the evidence ("reported to reduce", "has the potential to"). The example essay itself admits that further research on side effects and dosage is needed; that admission is the part worth imitating.
4. In the system/device essay, some supporting data comes from 2011-2019. Case counts and statistics must still be recent (5 years); older foundational scientific work may be used if the reason is written. [ADDED]
5. The examples in the photos are material for learning patterns, not official competition results. Do not call them "winning essays".

## Title types judges tend to like [ADDED]
These are patterns read from title-bank.md, review-cases.md, and the photos; not a guarantee of score. In order from the safest:
1. Named + form + material + problem + one goal: "[NAME]: [Form] from [MATERIAL] for [PROBLEM] to [one goal] in [TARGET]". The judge grasps the idea, material, and direction at once.
2. Based on local potential or waste: "Utilization of [LOCAL POTENTIAL] as [FORM] to [goal]". Reads as realistic and economical.
3. A program or system with scope: "Program/Method/Platform [NAME] to [goal] in [regency/community]". Suits social, legal, and education ideas.
4. A title that is a victim of its own creativity: a funny but ambiguous acronym, an expansion in parentheses, two goals, or a research word. Avoid all of them.
Every title still goes through the validation procedure in titles.md.
